    TIBETAN WHEEL · CYLINDER AND INSCRIPTION The inscription runs around the cylinder.

    FROM OBJECT TO PRAYER

    The scroll holds the mantra.
    Turning gives it rhythm.

    Inside a handheld mani wheel, a paper scroll inscribed many times with the mantra is wound around an axle. The same syllables are often engraved on the cylinder. Practitioners turn it while reciting aloud, in a whisper, or silently.

    In Tibetan Buddhism, turning a mani wheel is associated with the ritual recitation of the mantra it contains. The hand’s movement sustains the rhythm of prayer; a mālā can help keep count of repetitions, while circumambulating a stupa joins prayer with pilgrimage.

    MANTRA · TURNING · DEDICATION

    Three aspects of one prayer.

    Movement gives the hand a rhythm, the mantra recalls compassion, and dedication turns the practice toward the well-being of all beings. The object is simple; recitation and intention give the gesture its depth.

    01

    The rhythm of the hand

    Hold the wheel gently and let the cylinder turn at an even pace. If your mind wanders, return to the circle, the sound of the mantra, or your good intention—without rushing or chasing a record.

    ↗
    02

    The mantra of compassion

    “Om mani padme hum” is associated with Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion. Reciting it joins the sound of the mantra with the movement of the hand and recollection of compassion.

    ↗
    03

    Dedication to all beings

    At the close of the prayer, wish that whatever goodness arose in the practice may serve the happiness of all beings. In Mahayana Buddhism, this dedication of merit is an important concluding gesture.

    ↗

    In Tibetan tradition, a mani wheel is more than a vessel for a scroll. Its turning is associated with reciting Avalokiteshvara’s mantra, compassion, and dedicating the goodness of the practice to all beings.
