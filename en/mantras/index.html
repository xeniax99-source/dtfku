Buddhist mantras: what they are and why they are recited

A mantra is a sacred formula repeated aloud or silently in religious practice. Its pronunciation, explanation and method of practice depend on a particular Buddhist tradition and transmission.

WHAT IS CALLED A MANTRA?

Recitation as an anchor for attention

A mantra may be a short formula or a longer text. Repetition can steady attention, express devotion or call to mind the qualities of a buddha or bodhisattva. Not every mantra has a literal translation accepted by all schools; sound, ritual context and oral transmission also matter.

Some people count recitations on a mālā. Other practices combine mantra with visualization, prayer or dedication. No single form should be treated as the only correct one for all Buddhists.

CONTEXT MATTERS MORE THAN MECHANICAL COUNTING

Recite with care, without promises

A mantra is not a guarantee that a wish will come true or a medical treatment. Practitioners may join recitation with a kind intention, attention to their actions and care for others. Some tantric practices call for prior guidance or transmission; approaches differ, and not every mantra has the same requirements.

EXAMPLES AND CONTEXT

Three well-known mantras

Om Mani Padme Hum

Om mani padme hum

A six-syllable mantra especially associated in Tibetan Buddhism with Avalokiteshvara (Chenrezig), the bodhisattva of compassion.

Om Tare Tuttare Ture Svaha

Green Tara mantra

The formula “Om tare tuttare ture svaha” is associated with Tara practice; recitation forms depend on the lineage.

Tayatha Om Bekandze Bekandze Maha Bekandze Radza Samungate Soha

Medicine Buddha mantra

A formula associated with the Buddha Bhaisajyaguru. In Buddhist practice it may be recited as a prayer and focus for attention; it does not replace medical care.
