PRAYER · RECOLLECTION · GOOD INTENTIONS

Buddhist prayer: traditions, mantras and practices

There is no single form of prayer shared by every Buddhist school and culture. People may recite texts, repeat mantras, express aspirations, make offerings or dedicate the goodness of practice; the meaning depends on context.

DIFFERENT FORMS

Prayer may be an invocation or an aspiration

In some traditions, prayer includes addressing buddhas and bodhisattvas; elsewhere, reciting sutras, voicing an aspiration for awakening or wishing well to all beings may be central. Many practitioners combine prayer and meditation, though the terms are not interchangeable.

MANTRA AND RECOLLECTION

Recitation connects words with intention

A mantra may be recited aloud, in a whisper or silently, following the form used in a tradition. “Om mani padme hum”, for example, is associated with Avalokiteshvara and compassion. A mālā can help count recitations; a prayer wheel pairs mantra with repeated movement.

HEALTH · PROTECTION · WELL-BEING

A wish is not a guarantee

People may dedicate a prayer to someone who is ill, ask for protection in a difficult time, or wish for peace and well-being. Such intentions can support personal religious practice and a sense of connection; they do not guarantee outcomes. Prayer for health does not replace medical care, and a wish for protection does not replace sensible safety measures.
